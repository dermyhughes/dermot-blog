# Broader design references for “Just a few colours”

Researched 3 October 2026. The aim is to give the article's central observation—people reasonably take considered design for granted—its own references, while charts remain the running example. These sources offer design arguments and working practices; they do not constitute experimental proof that Aperture's proposed palette works.

## Editorial recommendation

The revised article uses three additions: Norman for the opening observation, Krause for the reader's expectations after filtering, and the Government Design Principles for making complex work usable and sharing its reasoning. Each reference connects to the existing chart example. Saffer, Rams and the GOV.UK contribution criteria remain useful alternatives if the article develops further.

Keep the current opening inside the reader's task. Introduce Norman after that scene, rather than making the article open with a quotation. Keep the humorous Electronics example: it gives the broader idea an ordinary consequence. Any language about accumulated judgement should name what is accumulated—research, decisions, limitations and guidance—instead of claiming that a tidy interface contains centuries of wisdom.

## 1. Don Norman: why successful design attracts less attention

**Primary source:** Don Norman, [Preface to *The Design of Everyday Things*, revised edition](https://jnd.org/preface-design-of-everyday-things-revised-edition/), published on his own website, 22 April 2013.

**Source statement:** Norman says considered design is harder to notice because it meets people's needs without requiring their attention; poor design makes its deficiencies conspicuous. The same preface says usability is only part of a product's experience: aesthetics, enjoyment and emotion also matter. [Source](https://jnd.org/preface-design-of-everyday-things-revised-edition/).

**Verified short extract:** “serving us without drawing attention to itself” (7 words). This is part of a longer sentence about good design, rather than a standalone maxim. [Source](https://jnd.org/preface-design-of-everyday-things-revised-edition/).

**Our application:** A stable category colour lets the person continue comparing reviews; the disappearing effort is their effort. Norman did not discuss the Electronics example or prescribe its allocation algorithm.

**Placement in the revision:** In the second opening paragraph, after the reader's scene. The linked book reference and brief quotation lead directly into the decisions that make a chart uneventful to use.

## 2. Rachel Krause / Nielsen Norman Group: expectations carry forward

**Primary source:** Rachel Krause, [Maintain Consistency and Adhere to Standards](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/consistency-and-standards/), Nielsen Norman Group, 10 January 2021. This is the organisation's explanation of Jakob Nielsen's own usability heuristic, rather than an aggregator's formulation.

**Source statement:** Krause distinguishes consistency within a product or family of products from conventions learned elsewhere. Both help people transfer what they already know. She also explains that consistency needs continuing review: a documented standard alone does not stop divergent interpretations. [Source](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/consistency-and-standards/).

**Our application:** The reader has learned that Electronics is purple, and filtering changes the visible categories rather than the meaning of that colour. Stable mappings preserve that understanding. This is our use of the principle, not a chart-specific result reported in Krause's article.

**Placement in the revision:** After the paragraph beginning “For the person reading it, that means a pause.” The discussion connects a familiar search icon to the more local expectation that Electronics remains purple. This broadens the example to learned conventions without introducing terminology the reader does not need.

**Caution:** Treat the heuristic as guidance. Avoid turning Jakob's Law into an experimentally established numerical claim or claiming all interfaces should be identical; Krause explicitly leaves room for context.

## 3. Dan Saffer: ordinary functionality can be overlooked

**Primary source:** [Designing Microinteractions](https://articles.centercentre.com/designing_microinteractions/), Center Centre's interview excerpt with Dan Saffer and Jared Spool, published 19 June 2013. The page is credited to Saffer and identifies the underlying podcast and transcript.

**Source statement:** Saffer describes small pieces of functionality through examples such as silencing a phone. People do not buy a phone for that action, but its absence causes problems. He discusses routine product functionality being neglected until late in development precisely because people expect it. [Source](https://articles.centercentre.com/designing_microinteractions/).

**Verified short extract:** “People just expect it to be there.” (7 words). The surrounding passage is Saffer's discussion of routine functionality. [Source](https://articles.centercentre.com/designing_microinteractions/).

**Our application:** Filtering is one of those small interactions whose behavioural rules matter. Do not imply that microinteractions mean decorative animation, or call the whole colour palette a microinteraction.

**Suggested placement:** An alternative to Krause after the filtering example, or a brief bridge from the opening into it. Saffer's phone example adds an everyday comparison if the chart scene needs widening. Using both Saffer and Krause in adjacent paragraphs is probably unnecessary.

## 4. Dieter Rams: the details are part of respecting the user

**Primary source:** [Ten principles for good design](https://www.vitsoe.com/gb/about/good-design), published by Vitsœ, the company that has worked with Rams on his furniture. The page dates the formulation of his principles to the late 1970s.

**Source statement:** Rams's eighth principle associates detailed care and accuracy with respect for users. His fifth principle describes purposeful products as tools whose restrained design leaves room for their users. These are different principles; avoid combining them into an invented Rams quotation. [Source](https://www.vitsoe.com/gb/about/good-design).

**Verified short extract:** “Good design is thorough down to the last detail” (9 words), the eighth principle's heading. [Source](https://www.vitsoe.com/gb/about/good-design).

**Our application:** Direct labels, stable identity and overflow handling are places where attention can save the reader extra work. Rams does not endorse any chart treatment in the draft.

**Suggested placement:** Optional near the end of “Category thirteen”, where a seemingly minor treatment affects whether someone can read the chart. Lower priority than Norman and the practical GOV.UK material: another famous maxim could make the post feel assembled from quotations.

## 5. GOV.UK Design System: shared components need evidence and context

**Primary source:** [Contribution criteria](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/community/contribution-criteria/), GOV.UK Design System, checked 3 October 2026.

**Source statement:** Before publication, components and patterns must demonstrate usability through research including disabled users, reuse relevant existing styles and components, and work across varied services and technology. The team says it will identify the contexts in which patterns were tested and invite feedback from other settings. [Source](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/community/contribution-criteria/).

**Our application:** A reusable palette should carry its tested contexts and limitations as well as values. Aperture can borrow this approach to evidence without suggesting GOV.UK's requirements govern Bazaarvoice or have already been met.

**Suggested placement:** Alongside the final discussion of handing off reasons and situations where the palette fails. This is concrete support for a design system as reusable decisions whose suitability still needs checking.

## 6. Government Design Principles: simplicity and shared reasoning

**Primary source:** [Government Design Principles](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles), Government Digital Service and Central Digital and Data Office. First published 3 April 2012; current page updated 2 April 2025.

**Source statement:** The fourth principle distinguishes a simple appearance from ease of use, especially when underlying systems are complex. The second advocates reuse of successful work; the ninth recommends sharing successful patterns together with their reasons, and changing them as needs or evidence change. [Source](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles).

**Verified short extract:** “Do the hard work to make it simple” (8 words), the fourth principle's heading. [Source](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-design-principles).

**Our application:** Stable category assignments are some of the work Aperture can resolve and pass on to teams. The principles do not prescribe chart colours or validate the twelve-colour proposal.

**Placement in the revision:** Near the ending, linking the user's uneventful interaction to a design system's responsibility to share decisions and keep them open to revision.

## 7. Eames Office: a less abstract example of small decisions serving customers

**Primary source:** Eames Office's [ECS (Eames Contact Storage)](https://www.eamesoffice.com/the-work/ecs-eames-contact-storage/) entry describes a 1961 film and its script. Its [Furniture Partners](https://www.eamesoffice.com/authenticity/furniture-partners/) page reproduces a related statement attributed specifically to Charles Eames. These are first-party archival sources, although the website's formulations differ.

**Source statement:** The film entry connects details of wire, timber, castings and joints to the way the finished product serves its customer. The partners page similarly discusses preserving the intended experience through manufacture and distribution. [Film entry](https://www.eamesoffice.com/the-work/ecs-eames-contact-storage/), [partners page](https://www.eamesoffice.com/authenticity/furniture-partners/).

**Our application:** The analogy is between components working together in a physical product and colour, labels and behaviour working together in a chart. It could support a wider historical reference, but requires explaining a second example.

**Suggested treatment:** Reserve rather than insert. The very familiar short slogan about details is reproduced in multiple variants. If used, paraphrase the concrete material example and cite the film entry; do not attribute an arbitrary online wording to Charles and Ray jointly.

## Sources and framings to leave out

- Quote sites, Reddit reposts and book PDFs on unrelated sites: Norman's own preface makes these unnecessary for attribution.
- Popular retellings of a supposedly ancient principle that good design is invisible: these blur an observation about unobtrusive use into a universal definition of quality. Norman's preface also discusses emotion and pleasure, so it does not support erasing those aspects.
- The Eames segmented-base table page was indexed with a useful connection example, but repeated direct fetches failed. The accessible archival film and partners entries above are preferable references for verifying the wording.
- Sweeping claims about centuries of design work contained in a chart: the sources establish earlier attention to detail and accumulated working practices, not that specific historical lineage.
- More theory words in the article: internal consistency, external consistency and microinteractions help us edit, but the reader mainly needs to understand why Electronics should stay purple.
