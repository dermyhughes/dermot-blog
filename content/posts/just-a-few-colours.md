<p>One of the designs I was shown early in my work on data visualisation for Aperture, our design system at Bazaarvoice, was a usage chart with dozens of lines. I think they were API keys. Each key had its own colour and a label made of random characters. There were so many that the legend needed pagination.</p>
<p>My first reaction was: why on earth would anyone design this?</p>
<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-width-wide"><img class="kg-image" src="/images/posts/data-visualisation-colours-api-key-overload.webp" width="1536" height="1024" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="Illustrative usage chart with usage count on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis. Dozens of coloured lines overlap and cross. A full-height legend on the right lists twenty random key identifiers with colour swatches; pagination reads 1 of 2 and 1–20 of 40."><figcaption>An illustrative reconstruction, with invented data and key labels. The legend has pages. The chart still has all the lines.</figcaption></figure>
<p>To follow a key, you have to find its name, remember its colour and pick its trail out of the tangle. The random strings give you little to recognise. Notice an interesting line first, and its name might be on another page of the legend. Even forty beautifully chosen colours would leave a lot of work for the reader.</p>
<p>That chart made the brief of choosing chart colours look rather less straightforward. A row of swatches in Figma, with matching tokens in code, would only cover part of it.</p>

<h2 id="fifty-colours-use-eight">Fifty colours. Use eight.</h2>
<p>I went looking at other design systems partly in the hope of borrowing an answer. <a href="https://atlassian.design/foundations/color/data-visualization-color">Atlassian offers eight categorical tokens and recommends showing five or six colours at once</a>. <a href="https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/">Carbon has an ordered palette of fourteen</a>. <a href="https://cloudscape.design/foundation/visual-foundation/data-vis-colors/">Cloudscape provides fifty</a>.</p>
<p>Fifty sounded generous. The same Cloudscape guidance recommends considering up to eight series in a line or bar chart, and five slices in a pie or donut. That was the more useful part of the answer for a chart whose legend already needed pages.</p>
<p>These are recommendations, rather than a fixed limit on what people can read. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2010.162">Research comparing displays with two, four and eight time series</a> found that adding series increased reading time and reduced accuracy. Which display worked best also depended on the comparison people were making.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/colors-for-data-vis-style-guides">Lisa Charlotte Muth&#39;s advice at Datawrapper</a> gave me a practical starting point: audit the charts your organisation actually makes. A thousand points might belong to a handful of categories. A request for forty colours might reveal a chart that needs fewer things competing for attention.</p>
<p>As an engineer, I was still drawn to generating colours. Define the qualities we want, let an algorithm search, and make the results repeatable. <a href="https://mattstromawn.com/writing/how-to-pick-the-least-wrong-colors/">Matt Ström&#39;s exploration of chart colours for Stripe</a> shows how that search can balance brand resemblance and distinction under colour-vision simulations.</p>
<p>It can help find candidates. Those candidates still need testing in charts: <a href="https://danielleszafir.com/colordiff_vis2017.pdf">colour discrimination changes with the size and shape of the marks</a>. I could generate colour thirty-seven and still leave someone struggling to follow line thirty-seven.</p>

<h2 id="electronics-unsolicited-rebrand">Electronics gets an unsolicited rebrand</h2>
<p>Reducing the view brings another decision into focus. Imagine a retail team comparing weekly review volumes across Clothing, Electronics and Home. Electronics is purple. They filter out Clothing, and Electronics turns orange.</p>
<p>The implementation is easy enough to explain. Colours were assigned by position in an array. Remove the first item and everything moves up a place. The chart still shows the right numbers, but Electronics has undergone an unsolicited rebrand.</p>
<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-width-wide"><img class="kg-image" src="/images/posts/data-visualisation-colours-04-filter-the-data-keep-the-identity.svg" width="1200" height="760" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="With zero-based palette slots, Clothing uses 00, Electronics 01 and Home 02. Filtering Clothing out shifts the remaining colours when allocation follows array positions. A stored category mapping keeps Electronics purple in slot 01 and Home teal in slot 02."><figcaption>A hypothetical example: remove Clothing and Electronics should stay purple.</figcaption></figure>
<p>For the reader, that means a pause. Wasn&#39;t Electronics purple? Has the selection changed? They check the legend and learn something they already knew.</p>
<p>Each category needs an assignment that survives filtering and sorting, shared across charts where people compare the same categories. Dark mode may need a different purple against its new background, but Electronics should remain recognisable. Looking at the finished swatches would tell you almost nothing about whether this behaviour had been considered.</p>

<h2 id="what-is-the-colour-saying">What is the colour saying?</h2>
<p>Even before anyone clicks a filter, the colours are making suggestions. If most of a dashboard is muted and one bar is bright red, I&#39;m going to look at the red bar first. If the product uses red for errors elsewhere, I may also assume something is wrong.</p>
<p>That&#39;s an awkward way to introduce the Home department.</p>
<p><a href="https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/">Carbon&#39;s guidance</a> separates the jobs colour can do: distinguish categories, show increasing amounts, show values either side of a midpoint, or signal a status. A darker heatmap cell might mean more reviews. A purple bar should help me find Electronics. A sentiment chart needs to distinguish positive and negative responses.</p>
<p>Asking for “colours for lots of data” conceals several different requests. Each needs a choice about what the reader should infer, including meanings we might accidentally suggest.</p>

<h2 id="category-thirteen">Category thirteen</h2>
<p>My starting proposal for Aperture was twelve curated colours. Twelve was a working number, to be tested against our product. I still needed an answer for the customer who adds a thirteenth category.</p>
<p>One option was to reuse the first blue with diagonal hatching, keeping the original category&#39;s solid fill. The example below extends that treatment to categories fourteen and fifteen.</p>
<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-width-wide"><img class="kg-image" src="/images/posts/data-visualisation-colours-09-scaling-13.svg" width="1200" height="1020" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="Proposed allocation for fifteen categories. The first twelve have solid fills. Categories thirteen, fourteen and fifteen reuse the first blue, orange and green with diagonal hatching. All fifteen bars have category labels and values."><figcaption>A proposed overflow treatment. Existing assignments stay put; additional categories reuse colours with a hatch. This still needs evaluation.</figcaption></figure>
<p>A bar may have room for a hatch; a tiny segment might swallow it. A line would need a different stroke, a scatter point perhaps a different shape. And if a dashed line already means “forecast”, it can&#39;t also mean “we ran out of colours”.</p>
<p>We can&#39;t wait until category thirteen to give people another way to read the chart. <a href="https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html">Colour alone mustn&#39;t carry the information</a>. These bars have direct labels from the outset. For this comparison, the labels and positions might make a single colour sufficient. The additional colours need to earn their place.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html">Contrast needs checking too</a>. A mark standing out against its background doesn&#39;t establish that someone can identify it among neighbouring marks. Nor does adding a pattern make an indefinitely crowded chart readable. Grouping, smaller comparable charts or a filter may help more. If a patterned category remains after filtering, it should keep its appearance.</p>

<h2 id="back-to-the-swatches">What we get to take for granted</h2>
<p>The proposal still includes a row of swatches: twelve candidate colours in light and dark themes, shared between Figma and code. Alongside them, Aperture needs rules for keeping identities stable, choosing additional cues and recognising when a chart has become too crowded.</p>
<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-width-wide"><img class="kg-image" src="/images/posts/data-visualisation-colours-10-curated-twelve-themes.svg" width="1200" height="752" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="Twelve fixed candidate colours in light and dark themes."><figcaption>Twelve candidate colours, with product and colour-vision evaluation still to come.</figcaption></figure>
<p>I want to see whether people can follow a series, identify a category without matching colours and keep their bearings after filtering. The results should change the proposal where it falls short, including the number of colours. Keyboard access, descriptions and <a href="https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/complex/">access to the underlying data</a> need the same care.</p>
<p>Don Norman describes good design as <a href="https://jnd.org/preface-design-of-everyday-things-revised-edition/">“serving us without drawing attention to itself”</a>. That&#39;s a useful ambition for this work. The next team should inherit the reasons and limitations alongside the tokens. The person checking their weekly reviews should be able to filter out Clothing, keep following Electronics and get back to the question that brought them there.</p>
