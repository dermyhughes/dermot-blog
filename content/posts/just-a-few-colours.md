<p>I was asked to look at introducing data visualisation colours into Aperture, our design system. Choose a palette, make sure it works, give it some tokens. A perfectly reasonable request.</p>
<p>I&#39;ve worked on design systems long enough to be slightly suspicious of those.</p>
<p>A tooltip sounds reasonable too, right up until you&#39;re discussing keyboard focus, portals and which layer of the application it should live in. The component is small. The number of things it needs to get along with is another matter.</p>
<p>I had a feeling chart colours might go the same way. Still, there was an obvious place to start. We already had colour primitives and semantic tokens: the values themselves, then names describing what they were for. I could picture a tidy row of swatches in Figma, ready for the next team to use.</p>
<p>That was the outcome I wanted. I needed to work out what would make it dependable.</p>
<p>My first instinct was to look for colours with enough contrast. But “enough contrast” needed a little unpacking. Against the background? Against the next segment in a stacked bar? Enough difference to match a line to its legend, then follow it through several other lines?</p>
<p>I couldn&#39;t choose sensible values until I knew what the colours were meant to communicate.</p>
<p>The research helped separate the jobs hidden inside the request. <a href="https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/">Carbon&#39;s guidance</a> distinguishes categorical colours for different identities, sequential scales for increasing amounts, diverging scales for values either side of a midpoint, and palettes with established meanings such as status.</p>
<p>That gave me a more useful starting point. A product category needs an identity. A volume needs a progression. A failure needs a meaning people recognise. Borrowing the error red for whichever category comes next might be convenient, but it could also suggest the entire department needed somebody&#39;s attention.</p>
<p>Related measures need consideration too. Actuals and forecasts might share a colour, with a dashed line or patterned fill distinguishing the forecast. <a href="https://www.sap.com/design-system/sac-dashboard-design-best-practices/resources/color-palettes">SAP uses related treatments for different reporting roles</a>. So even the decision to give two things different colours depends on the relationship we want to preserve.</p>
<p>Once those roles were separated, the categorical case was the one that worried me most. We could define a scale for volume. We couldn&#39;t know every category a customer might introduce. How many distinct colours would we need, and who would keep choosing them?</p>
<p>I went looking at other systems, partly for guidance and partly in the hope that somebody had already found an elegant answer I could borrow.</p>
<p><a href="https://atlassian.design/foundations/color/data-visualization-color">Atlassian provides eight categorical tokens and recommends five or six colours in a visualisation</a>. <a href="https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/">Carbon provides an ordered fourteen-colour palette</a>. Then I reached <a href="https://cloudscape.design/foundation/visual-foundation/data-vis-colors/">Cloudscape: fifty categorical colours</a>.</p>
<p>Fifty sounded promising. Its guidance also recommends up to eight series in a line or bar chart, and five slices in a pie or donut.</p>
<p>That qualification changed how I understood the problem. A large palette gives teams more choices across a product. It doesn&#39;t make every one of those choices distinguishable in the same chart. Even the system with fifty colours was telling people to show fewer of them.</p>
<p>It also helped separate data volume from visual complexity. A thousand points might share a handful of category identities. A chart with far fewer marks could still ask someone to untangle too many series. <a href="https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/colors-for-data-vis-style-guides">Lisa Charlotte Muth&#39;s advice at Datawrapper</a> points to the practical way of deciding what matters: audit the charts your organisation actually makes.</p>
<p>The mock dashboard below illustrates these distinctions. It was created for this article using invented review data. The scatter plot shares eight colours across 240 products; the heatmap uses one ordered ramp; sentiment has its own meaning. The eight-slice donut illustrates a case where Cloudscape&#39;s guidance would encourage fewer slices.</p>

<p>The research had made a finite palette feel like a reasonable direction. It left me with the maintenance question. Those colours would need to work across themes, and any extension would need to fit what was already there. Could generation help us choose and maintain that set?</p>
<p>As an engineer, I could see the appeal. Define the qualities we want, let an algorithm explore the possibilities, and keep the inputs fixed so the output is repeatable. That last part matters when screenshots are part of your release process.</p>
<p>First, though, I needed useful controls over the colours themselves.</p>
<p>HSL describes hue, saturation and lightness, but its lightness setting can be misleading. The <a href="https://www.w3.org/TR/css-color-4/#the-hsl-notation">CSS Color specification uses a simple example</a>: pure yellow and pure blue both have HSL lightness of 50%, yet the yellow looks much lighter. We&#39;re comparing how light they look here, not which grabs your attention. A dark blue can stand out strongly against a white page.</p>
<p>OKLCH offered a better starting point. Built on <a href="https://bottosson.github.io/posts/oklab/">Björn Ottosson&#39;s Oklab colour model</a>, it gives us a lightness coordinate designed to reflect perception, alongside chroma and hue. In the second pair below, the yellow and blue hues are set to the same OKLCH lightness. Their lightness is much more closely balanced.</p>
<p>The grey versions make the comparison easier to inspect. Each preserves its colour&#39;s relative luminance, so the first pair becomes an almost-white grey and a dark grey. The second pair produces two much closer greys. That is the sort of difference a shared HSL number conceals.</p>

<p>That made systematic exploration more appealing. We could hold some qualities steady while changing others, or keep a chosen brand hue while exploring lightness and chroma. But a search still needs a definition of a good result.</p>
<p><a href="https://mattstromawn.com/writing/how-to-pick-the-least-wrong-colors/">Matt Ström&#39;s work on chart colours for Stripe</a> helped here. His approach explores competing goals, including resemblance to a chosen palette and distinction under colour-vision simulations. The choices about what matters remain design decisions, even when an algorithm does the searching.</p>
<p>We&#39;d also need to check the final colours after any adjustment to fit the range our output supports, called its <em>gamut</em>. The <a href="https://www.w3.org/TR/css-color-4/">CSS Color specification</a> describes that mapping. The values delivered to the chart are the ones that have to work.</p>
<p>This was where my enthusiasm for generation acquired some boundaries. Better controls could help us find candidates. They couldn&#39;t guarantee that someone could follow a thin line through seven others. Keep adding colours and the distinctions get harder, however reliably the generator produces them.</p>
<p>At least the screenshots would be consistent.</p>
<p>That left a more focused question. If we curate a fixed set of colours, what should happen when the data needs one more identity than the palette provides?</p>
<p>I was leaning towards twelve authored colours. Once those were allocated, the next category could reuse the first colour with a different treatment. For filled marks, that would mean a pattern: solid blue for category 1, blue with diagonal hatching for category 13. The hue repeats, but the complete visual identity doesn&#39;t.</p>
<p>This is a form of automation I could see us owning. We&#39;d define the palette and a small vocabulary of treatments. The system would allocate the combinations predictably, without asking a designer to invent colour 13, then 14, then 15.</p>
<p>There was an important distinction to get right before turning that into a rule. Running out of colours is one reason to add a pattern. Accessibility has to be considered before that happens.</p>
<p>The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, or WCAG, address both <a href="https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html">information conveyed through colour alone</a> and <a href="https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html">the contrast of graphical objects</a>. Relevant graphical parts generally need at least 3:1 against adjacent colours, with exceptions. Passing a background-contrast check doesn&#39;t ensure that someone can distinguish every category.</p>
<p>So the first twelve would still need a way to identify the information without relying on hue: directly labelled bars, for example. A legend that only maps coloured squares to names leaves the reader needing to recognise those colours. Some charts may need additional cues well before twelve. The palette boundary is an allocation rule, not a point at which accessibility switches on.</p>
<p>Within that baseline, the approach has support. <a href="https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/general/G111">W3C documents colour-plus-pattern encoding</a>, and both <a href="https://www.highcharts.com/docs/chart-design-and-style/pattern-fills">Highcharts</a> and <a href="https://www.chartjs.org/docs/latest/general/colors.html#patterns-and-gradients">Chart.js</a> support pattern fills. Those sources establish the technique and its implementation possibilities. They don&#39;t prescribe twelve as the threshold for introducing it.</p>
<p>Here&#39;s the progression I mean. This example was made for the article, with invented values. At twelve categories, every fill is solid. Add category 13 and only that new category receives the patterned treatment. At twenty-four, the same twelve colours have each been used twice.</p>

<p>This also gives us room to keep some personality in the palette. I&#39;d curate suitable brand colours and supporting hues, with separate values for light and dark surfaces. Electric blue can remain electric blue when we reuse it; the additional distinction comes from its treatment. We don&#39;t have to keep finding increasingly similar shades just to make the array longer.</p>
<p>The treatment would depend on the chart. Hatching has room to work in a filled bar. A thin line needs a different stroke style; a scatter point might need a different shape. We would choose the cue that fits the mark, rather than require every series to carry a pattern, marker and dash at once.</p>

Each example is directly labelled."><figcaption>Alternatives for rendering the first repeated hue. <p>I would keep this policy specific to categorical identity. An ordered heatmap still needs its progression, and sentiment still needs its established meaning. We&#39;d also have to reserve patterns that already mean something else, such as forecast data. A hatch can&#39;t quietly acquire two different jobs in the same chart.</p>
<p>There is a limit to what the extra treatment buys us. If two solid colours are hard to distinguish, giving both a matching diagonal pattern on the second pass won&#39;t fix that relationship. Direct identification still matters. Tiny segments may not show enough of a pattern to recognise it at all.</p>
<p>And the guidance from other systems still applies: twelve colours and two treatments don&#39;t make a twenty-four-line chart a good idea. Sometimes the next category should prompt a filter, a grouped view or smaller comparable charts. I&#39;d set a supported capacity deliberately and define what happens when it is exceeded. I wouldn&#39;t just keep cycling through stripes until the dashboard resembled a carpet catalogue.</p>
<p>That gives us a proposal to evaluate with actual product charts and real tasks. Readable text, keyboard access for interactions, descriptions and access to the underlying data also remain part of the work, as <a href="https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/complex/">W3C&#39;s guidance on complex images</a> makes clear.</p>
<p>Automatic reuse brought the stability question into sharper focus. Once a category had received its colour and treatment, both needed to survive the next interaction.</p>
<p>I&#39;d already been thinking about stable output from the generator. But the palette could remain unchanged while the application changed which colour belonged to which category. Repeatable builds wouldn&#39;t prevent that.</p>
<p>Here&#39;s a hypothetical example. Germany is purple in a chart comparing countries. Someone removes France with a filter, and Germany becomes orange. We assigned colours by position in an array, and everything moved up a place. Germany has undergone an unsolicited rebrand.</p>

<p>The fix belongs in the assignment rules. Tokens should provide reusable colours and treatments. The application needs to map stable category IDs to those combinations, preserve the assignments when data is filtered or reordered, and share them across related charts. Category 13 should keep its hatch even if a filter leaves only three categories visible. Counting the visible rows again would undo the stability we wanted.</p>
<p>Turning an ID into a repeatable number—a technique called hashing—could help choose a slot. But two IDs can still land in the same one. Generating a hue directly from each name has a similar limitation: the colours that happen to appear together may be difficult to distinguish. I&#39;d keep hashing as a possible assignment fallback, with a defined response to collisions.</p>
<p>That separation between identity and value also gives us a way to handle themes. Germany can keep its purple identity while the actual colour changes to suit a dark surface. <a href="https://atlassian.design/foundations/color/data-visualization-color">Atlassian&#39;s theme-aware chart tokens</a> use different light and dark values. We&#39;d need to review those values in complete charts, including selected and muted states.</p>
<p>By this point, I had a clearer answer to what we would need to ship: <strong>a curated twelve-colour palette, with automatic, stable reuse through additional treatments when its colours are exhausted</strong>.</p>
<p>Twelve is a starting proposal to test against our product, not a number established by the research. I&#39;d author and review every colour in both themes, then publish the complete set to Figma and code. Colour-generation tools could still help during that authoring process. The delivered palette would be fixed.</p>

<p>The automation would live in assignment: choose an available colour-and-treatment combination for a new category, store that choice and render it appropriately for the chart. The first reused fill gets a hatch; the original solid fill stays solid. Filtering or sorting wouldn&#39;t reshuffle either one.</p>
<p>That is a smaller responsibility than asking a generator to keep inventing distinguishable colours. It also has an honest boundary. We can calculate how many combinations the system can allocate. We still have to establish how many a particular chart can use well.</p>
<p>After all that, the approach I&#39;d choose is fairly straightforward: twelve curated colours with some personality, considered separately for light and dark. When the palette runs out, reuse those colours with an additional treatment suited to the chart. Keep the complete identities stable. Simplify the view when the number of categories stops being useful.</p>
<p>It&#39;s an answer I can explain in a few sentences. Getting to those sentences took considerably longer.</p>
<p>Each question changed something about the answer. Perception changed how I approached choosing colours. Accessibility changed what I expected colour to carry. Scale changed how I thought about reuse. Filtering revealed that even a carefully chosen palette could lose its meaning through a perfectly ordinary interaction.</p>
<p>The research gave me reasons for the choices and a clearer understanding of their limits. It also helped me decide where to stop. An endless supply of generated colours had sounded useful. A finite set with deliberate rules felt like something we could understand, maintain and use.</p>
<p>That&#39;s a familiar shape of work in design systems. A small request touches several things, each of which deserves some thought. Follow those connections far enough and you can turn the decisions into defaults, examples and guidance that other teams can build on.</p>
<p>The next person making a chart shouldn&#39;t have to rediscover why their yellow looks lighter, why Germany changes colour after filtering, or what happens when category thirteen arrives.</p>
<p>I started by imagining a tidy row of swatches in Figma. I still think that&#39;s a good outcome.</p>
<p>Ideally, the next team opens it, finds a few good colours, and wonders what all the fuss was about.</p>
