<p>I was asked to look at introducing data visualisation colours into Aperture, our design system. Choose a palette, make sure it works, give it some tokens. A perfectly reasonable request.</p>
<p>I&#39;ve worked on design systems long enough to be slightly suspicious of those.</p>
<p>A tooltip sounds reasonable too, right up until you&#39;re discussing keyboard focus, portals and which layer of the application it should live in. The component is small. The number of things it needs to get along with is another matter.</p>
<p>Still, we already had colour primitives and semantic tokens: the values themselves, then names describing what they were for. I could picture a tidy row of swatches in Figma, ready for the next team to use.</p>
<p>Then I started asking what would happen when somebody actually used them.</p>

<h2 id="what-is-the-colour-saying">What is the colour saying?</h2>
<p>My first instinct was to look for enough contrast. Against the background? Against the next segment in a stacked bar? Enough difference to follow a line through seven others?</p>
<p>Before choosing values, I needed to know what the colours were meant to communicate. <a href="https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/">Carbon&#39;s guidance</a> helped separate the jobs: categorical colours distinguish identities, sequential scales show increasing amounts, diverging scales show values either side of a midpoint, and status colours carry established meanings.</p>
<p>A product category needs an identity. A volume needs a progression. A failure needs a meaning people recognise. Borrowing the error red for whichever category comes next might be convenient, but it could also suggest the entire department needed somebody&#39;s attention.</p>
<p>Those jobs put different demands on a palette. The categorical case worried me most. We could define a scale for volume. We couldn&#39;t know every category a customer might introduce.</p>
<p>How many colours were we signing up to provide?</p>

<h2 id="fifty-colours-use-eight">Fifty colours. Use eight.</h2>
<p>I went looking at other systems, partly for guidance and partly in the hope that somebody had already found an elegant answer I could borrow.</p>
<p><a href="https://atlassian.design/foundations/color/data-visualization-color">Atlassian provides eight categorical tokens and recommends five or six colours in a visualisation</a>. <a href="https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/color-palettes/">Carbon provides an ordered fourteen-colour palette</a>. Then I reached <a href="https://cloudscape.design/foundation/visual-foundation/data-vis-colors/">Cloudscape: fifty categorical colours</a>.</p>
<p>Fifty sounded promising.</p>
<p>The same guidance recommends up to eight series in a line or bar chart, and five slices in a pie or donut.</p>
<p>That was the first useful surprise. Even the system with fifty colours was telling people to show fewer of them. A large palette gives teams choices across a product; it doesn&#39;t give the reader a greater ability to tell them apart in one chart.</p>
<p>It also exposed a trap in the phrase “lots of data”. A thousand points might share a handful of category identities. Twenty lines might be a nightmare. Counting records wouldn&#39;t tell me how many colours we needed. <a href="https://www.datawrapper.de/blog/colors-for-data-vis-style-guides">Lisa Charlotte Muth&#39;s advice at Datawrapper</a> was more practical: audit the charts your organisation actually makes.</p>
<p>This mock dashboard, made for the article with invented review data, shows the distinction. The scatter plot shares eight colours across 240 products. The heatmap needs one ordered ramp. Sentiment has its own meaning. Meanwhile, the eight-slice donut is already beyond Cloudscape&#39;s recommendation.</p>

It illustrates colour roles and the difference between the number of data points and the number of category identities.</figcaption></figure>
<p>I was beginning to see a finite palette as a sensible choice. The next temptation was to find a clever way to make it.</p>

<h2 id="surely-we-can-generate-them">Surely we can generate them</h2>
<p>As an engineer, I could see the appeal. Define the qualities we want, let an algorithm explore the possibilities, and keep the inputs fixed so the output is repeatable. That last part matters when screenshots are part of your release process.</p>
<p>But even the controls needed scrutiny. HSL gives us hue, saturation and lightness. Set pure yellow and pure blue to 50% lightness and they ought to look about equally light, surely?</p>
<p><a href="https://www.w3.org/TR/css-color-4/#the-hsl-notation">The CSS Color specification uses exactly that example</a> to show why they don&#39;t. Yellow looks much lighter. The numbers agree; your eyes have objections.</p>
<p>OKLCH offered more useful controls. Built on <a href="https://bottosson.github.io/posts/oklab/">Björn Ottosson&#39;s Oklab colour model</a>, it has a lightness coordinate designed around perception, alongside chroma and hue. In the second pair below, yellow and blue share the same OKLCH lightness and look much more closely balanced. The grey versions, which preserve relative luminance, make the difference easier to inspect.</p>
<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-width-wide"><img class="kg-image" src="/images/posts/data-visualisation-colours-02-equal-numbers-different-colours.svg" width="1200" height="1020" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="Top: yellow and blue both have HSL lightness 50%, but their luminance-preserving greys are very different. Bottom: yellow and blue at OKLCH lightness 0.65 and chroma 0.12 produce much closer greys."><figcaption>A calculated illustration based on W3C&#39;s yellow/blue example. Greys preserve linear-light sRGB relative luminance, with 8-bit rounding. Luminance is not a complete model of perceived lightness; the second pair is close, not identical. HSL and OKLCH use different lightness scales. These samples explain the controls, rather than propose a chart palette.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Now we could hold some qualities steady while exploring others. Keep a brand hue, vary its lightness and chroma, look for useful candidates. <a href="https://mattstromawn.com/writing/how-to-pick-the-least-wrong-colors/">Matt Ström&#39;s work on chart colours for Stripe</a> shows how an algorithm can search across competing goals, including resemblance to a chosen palette and distinction under colour-vision simulations.</p>
<p>The awkward part is deciding what counts as a good result. A pair of colours can look comfortably different as large swatches and become difficult to distinguish as thin, crossing lines. We would still need to evaluate the final colours in the charts people use.</p>
<p>Generation could help us author a palette. It couldn&#39;t make an unlimited supply of distinguishable colours. I could build something that reliably produced colour number thirty-seven without making line number thirty-seven any easier to follow.</p>
<p>At least the screenshots would be consistent.</p>

<h2 id="category-thirteen">Category thirteen</h2>
<p>I was leaning towards twelve authored colours, reviewed in light and dark themes. Twelve was a starting proposal to test against our product, rather than a number the research had established.</p>
<p>Then a customer adds a thirteenth category.</p>
<p>We could go looking for another shade. But that would reopen the same problem at fourteen, then fifteen. Instead, I wanted to try reusing the first colour with a different treatment: solid blue for category 1, blue with diagonal hatching for category 13.</p>
<p>The hue repeats. The complete visual identity doesn&#39;t.</p>
<p>This was automation I could see us owning. We would curate the colours and a small vocabulary of treatments, then allocate the combinations predictably. Electric blue could stay electric blue; we wouldn&#39;t have to keep diluting the palette to make the array longer.</p>
<p>There is a catch worth dealing with immediately: the first twelve still need to work for someone who can&#39;t distinguish their colours. Accessibility doesn&#39;t begin when the palette runs out.</p>
<p>WCAG addresses both <a href="https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html">information conveyed through colour alone</a> and <a href="https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html">the contrast of graphical objects</a>. Relevant graphical parts generally need at least 3:1 against adjacent colours, with exceptions. Passing a background-contrast check doesn&#39;t establish that every category can be identified.</p>
<p>For these bars, direct labels do that work from the first category. A legend that only maps coloured squares to names would leave the reader with the same colour-matching problem. Other charts may need shapes, patterns or line styles from the outset.</p>
<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-width-wide"><img class="kg-image" src="/images/posts/data-visualisation-colours-09-scaling-13.svg" width="1200" height="1020" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="Scaling demonstration at thirteen categories: category 13 reuses the first blue with diagonal hatching. The original twelve colour-and-fill assignments remain unchanged."><figcaption>The first reused colour gains a pattern. No new hex value is generated, and the existing categories keep their identities.</figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/general/G111">Colour plus pattern is an established technique</a>. The proposed rule here is about when to reuse a colour, and how to make that reuse predictable. It needs to fit the mark: hatching has room to work in a filled bar, a line can use a different stroke, and a scatter point can use a different shape.</p>
<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-width-wide"><img class="kg-image" src="/images/posts/data-visualisation-colours-11-overflow-by-chart.svg" width="1200" height="522" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="Category 01 and category 13 share the same blue. Filled marks distinguish them with solid fill and hatching; line examples use solid and dashed strokes; point examples use circles and squares. Each example is directly labelled."><figcaption>Alternatives for rendering the first repeated hue. These samples explain the proposed rule; each treatment still needs evaluation at the chart&#39;s actual size.</figcaption></figure>
<p>Those treatments have meanings too. If a dashed line already means “forecast”, it can&#39;t also quietly mean “we ran out of colours”. This policy would be for categorical identity; ordered heatmaps and status colours would keep their own rules.</p>
<p>Nor does a second pass magically double readability. If two solid colours are hard to distinguish, adding the same hatch to both won&#39;t fix that relationship. Tiny segments may not show enough of a pattern to recognise it at all.</p>
<p>Twenty-four combinations would give us twenty-four assignments. Whether a chart should show all of them is a separate decision. Sometimes the next category should prompt a filter, a grouped view or smaller comparable charts. I wouldn&#39;t keep cycling through stripes until the dashboard resembled a carpet catalogue.</p>
<p>But suggesting a filter brought me to the problem I found most revealing.</p>

<h2 id="germanys-unsolicited-rebrand">Germany&#39;s unsolicited rebrand</h2>
<p>Imagine Germany is purple in a chart comparing countries. Someone removes France with a filter, and Germany becomes orange.</p>
<p>Nothing about Germany has changed. We assigned colours by position in an array, and everything moved up a place. Germany has undergone an unsolicited rebrand.</p>
<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-width-wide"><img class="kg-image" src="/images/posts/data-visualisation-colours-04-filter-the-data-keep-the-identity.svg" width="1200" height="760" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="With zero-based palette slots, France uses 00, Germany 01 and Spain 02. Filtering France out shifts the remaining colours when allocation follows array positions. A stored category mapping keeps Germany purple in slot 01 and Spain teal in slot 02."><figcaption>A hypothetical filtering interaction, with palette slots beginning at 00 as in a JavaScript array. The stored mapping preserves each remaining category&#39;s colour when France is removed from view.</figcaption></figure>
<p>I&#39;d been thinking about stable output from a generator. Here, every colour value could remain exactly the same while the chart changed what those colours meant.</p>
<p>The reader had learned something useful: purple is Germany. One ordinary interaction had made that knowledge wrong. No improvement to the purple itself would fix this.</p>
<p>The application would need to map stable category IDs to colour-and-treatment combinations, preserve those assignments through filtering and sorting, and share them across related charts. Category 13 should keep its hatch even if a filter leaves only three categories visible. Its identity belongs to the category, not its current position on screen.</p>
<p>That also clarifies what should happen in dark mode. Germany can keep its purple identity while the actual value changes to suit the surface, as <a href="https://atlassian.design/foundations/color/data-visualization-color">Atlassian&#39;s theme-aware chart tokens</a> allow. The assignment stays put; the rendering adapts.</p>
<p>The automation I wanted had changed. I&#39;d started by imagining a system that could keep inventing colours. Now I wanted one that remembered its decisions.</p>

<h2 id="back-to-the-swatches">Back to the swatches</h2>
<p>The proposal I&#39;d take forward is <strong>twelve curated colours, with stable reuse through additional treatments when the colours are exhausted</strong>. Author and review the values in both themes, then publish the same set to Figma and code. Generation tools could help during authoring; the delivered palette would be fixed.</p>
<p>These are illustrative candidates, with the first eight used in the mock dashboard above. Product and colour-vision evaluation still lie ahead.</p>
<figure class="kg-card kg-image-card kg-width-wide"><img class="kg-image" src="/images/posts/data-visualisation-colours-10-curated-twelve-themes.svg" width="1200" height="752" loading="lazy" decoding="async" alt="Twelve fixed candidate colours in light and dark themes. Every value is part of the curated palette. Overflow reuses these colours with an additional treatment instead of generating more colours."><figcaption>Illustrative candidates selected through the preceding exploration, not approved brand tokens. All twelve values are fixed inputs in this proposal. Their stated surface contrast has been checked; product and colour-vision evaluation remain to be done.</figcaption></figure>
<p>The next step would be to test the proposal with real charts and real tasks: can someone follow a series, identify a category and keep their bearings after filtering? That would help establish the supported capacity and when we should simplify the view. Readable text, keyboard access for interactions, descriptions and <a href="https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/complex/">access to the underlying data</a> remain part of the work.</p>
<p>It&#39;s an answer I can explain in a few sentences. Getting to those sentences took considerably longer.</p>
<p>What surprised me was how much of the useful work happened beyond the swatches. Choosing a purple mattered. So did deciding what it meant, what happened when we reused it, and whether it still meant the same thing after someone clicked a filter.</p>
<p>Those decisions are what I want Aperture to carry for the next team. They should be able to make a chart without rediscovering why their yellow looks lighter, why Germany changes colour, or what happens when category thirteen arrives.</p>
<p>I started by imagining a tidy row of swatches in Figma. I still think that&#39;s a good outcome.</p>
<p>Ideally, the next team opens it, finds a few good colours, and wonders what all the fuss was about.</p>
